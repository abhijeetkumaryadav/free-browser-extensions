What is a launch window?

A launch window is the brief period when a rocket can lift off and still reach its intended orbit. It exists because the launch site is rotating with the Earth, and the target orbit is a fixed ring in space — the two only line up at certain times.

Orbital plane alignment

Think of an orbit as a tilted hula-hoop around Earth. Your launch site sits on the spinning surface. A launch window opens when Earth's rotation carries the pad directly under the target orbit's plane, so the rocket can fly straight into it instead of burning extra fuel to turn.

Why windows are short

For a space station rendezvous, alignment may last only minutes — the station whips overhead at 7.66 km/s. For geostationary or interplanetary targets the geometry repeats daily or on longer cycles, so windows can be hours or days long.

The plane-change penalty

Miss the window and you must change the orbital plane after launch. Plane changes are the most expensive maneuver in rocketry — changing direction at orbital speed can cost more fuel than reaching orbit in the first place. That's why timing matters.

Key terms

  • Inclination — the tilt of the orbit relative to the equator.
  • Ascending node — where the orbit crosses the equator going north.
  • Launch azimuth — the compass direction the rocket flies after liftoff.
  • Instantaneous window — a window with zero slack: launch on the second, or wait.
